Reliability & SLA Tagging Framework HC and intern reliability tags, when to apply them

Purpose: consistent, transparent tracking of Host Company and Intern reliability through standardized tags — guides operational decisions, placement eligibility, and escalation. All tagging happens in Moo, with supporting notes in HubSpot.

Core principles

  • Consistency — apply tags using the same criteria across all placements.
  • Transparency — clearly communicate tagging reasons and next steps.
  • Documentation — factual, evidence-based records in HubSpot.
  • Early intervention — escalate promptly at the first sign of recurring or severe issues.

Process for applying tags

  • 1. Identify: review WFL data, evaluations, communication history. Classify minor, recurring, or severe.
  • 2. Confirm evidence: date, summary, impact, supporting links (Slack, email, WFLs).
  • 3. Apply tag in Moo with a contextual note on rationale and next steps.
  • 4. Communicate: SLA 1/2 → factual summary to the relevant party. Termination → follow Internship Change Guidelines. Previously Blacklisted HC re-engaging → mandatory review call before any new intern, no assignment before that call, document call summary and decision in HubSpot.
  • 5. Review: reassess quarterly or at program end. HCs return to Reliable after three consecutive successful placements or verified improvement.

Documentation standards

  • Minimum evidence: emails, WFLs, evaluations, texts. Record date, description, affected parties, impact.
  • Retain evidence for the full placement cycle + 6 months for audit.
  • All intervention/re-engagement calls need summaries linked in HubSpot.

Tagging cadence

  • Weekly: update SLA 1 if resolved/escalated; add new evidence for SLA 2.
  • Event-driven: update the tag immediately with date and evidence when a new incident occurs.
  • Quarterly / program-end: reassess all tags, confirm Reliable eligibility, escalate or de-escalate as needed.
Host Company Blacklist Process Trigger events, decision flow, system steps

Purpose: a clear, consistent framework for identifying, evaluating, and blacklisting host companies during active internships when their actions pose risk to interns or VI — evidence-based, collaborative (PSA + PM), protective.

Key roles

  • PSA: first point of contact. Monitors WFLs and feedback, documents incidents, initiates pushback, drafts POAs, prepares escalation reports.
  • PM: reviews evidence with PSA. Co-leads the decision to blacklist, issue a final warning, or continue with stricter terms.

1. Trigger events

  • Safety & legal: harassment, discrimination, unsafe conditions, fraud, illegal activity, breach of contract.
  • Severe SLA violations: persistent onboarding failure despite intervention; 3+ missed WFLs with no communication; refusal to complete evaluations after multiple reminders.
  • Unprofessional conduct: rude, abusive, or inappropriate communication toward interns or VI staff.
  • Negative internship impact: multiple interns report no meaningful tasks, unethical treatment, or poor mentorship across consecutive cycles.

2. Initial assessment (PSA)

  • Document the incident in HubSpot with dates, descriptions, evidence.
  • Post and pin a summary tagging the relevant PMs.
  • Classify severity: Minor / Major / Critical. Critical cases skip straight to the decision meeting.

3. Intervention & pushback phase

Required before blacklisting unless Critical: HC conversation using termination pushback scripts → POA with agreed steps and deadlines → 2–4 week monitoring period.

Outcomes: resolved → close case, flag HC "At Risk – Monitored" for next cycle. Unresolved → decision meeting.

4. Decision meeting (PSA + PM)

Prepare a case summary (timeline, evidence, prior attempts) → review collectively → decide Blacklist / Final Warning / Re-engage under stricter terms → log the decision in Moo and HubSpot.

5. System updates (PSA)

  • HC profile in Moo → "Blacklist" → ignore any active-process warning and proceed.
  • Choose the correct reason code (competitor, poor feedback, unresponsive, disrespectful, off-platform activity, etc.) or "Other" with free-text notes (date, reason, decision summary, key attachments).
  • Submit.

6. Post-blacklist follow-up

  • Currently-placed interns → transition or monitor closely to completion.
  • Review all blacklisted HCs every 6 months for continued appropriateness.

Default approach: no proactive notification

We don't proactively tell an HC they've been blacklisted — this avoids unnecessary conflict and gives VI control over timing and framing. The HC naturally loses dashboard visibility as part of the process.

If the HC reaches out

  • Acknowledge calmly and professionally, neutral tone throughout.
  • Give factual, non-inflammatory feedback focused on program standards, intern safety, and best-practice alignment.
  • Never assign blame or use emotionally charged language.
  • PMs and PSA align internally on future participation; all notes and decisions documented in HubSpot so QA can proceed on offer reviews.
  • If re-engagement is approved: supervised trial period → on success, remove blacklist status and apply "Previously Blacklisted" tag for tracking.

Simone can be consulted for complex or high-stakes cases but isn't required for standard decisions.

Internship Change Guidelines When a change is warranted, RESTART vs RESUME, timelines

A change is not the default solution — only after reasonable efforts at coaching, mediation, and realignment. Interns dismissed from an HC get one replacement only, at the team's discretion.

General rules

  • CF/role changes during an HC change need PRS sign-off and suitable skills.
  • Hours or application-field adjustments need PMS approval before the change, within batch, discussed with PAM.
  • Each placement is a separate internship — max 16 weeks per placement. Prior placement time doesn't count toward the new duration.

Not sufficient on their own

  • "Don't like the work" / find it boring · no daily tasks (interns are expected to be proactive) · timezone mismatch unless truly incompatible · small/early-stage company · communication or cultural differences without disrespect.

Request categorization

Reason typeImmediate change?Path
Disrespect / misconductYesEscalate to PMS immediately, log in Moo. No mediation — misconduct overrides it. Change immediately; blacklist HC if confirmed.
Severe misalignmentNoConfirm tasks vs offer letter → attempt realignment, 7-day review → change if realignment fails.
Unresponsiveness (10+ days)NoExecute SLA outreach → final 2-day grace period → change + flag if no reply.
Mutual disengagementNoIntake reasons, probe deeper → mediation call within 3 days → change if unresolved.
Intern discontentNoCoach on proactive behavior, discuss alternate projects with HC. No change unless it escalates.
Host discontentNoSupport intern with coaching, 7–10 day review. If persists → possible withdrawal, not change.
Timezone / culture mismatchNoProvide async tools and structured calls, 7–10 day review. Change only if truly unworkable.
Company size / structureNoSet realistic expectations, monitor only. Grounds for change only if tied to misconduct or misalignment.

Process

  • 1. Intake: log in HubSpot, acknowledge within 1 business day, collect detailed reasons from both parties, check background (PMS/PRS notes, SLA tags, offer vs actual tasks).
  • 2. Categorize using the table above.
  • 3. Investigate: clarifying questions, look for miscommunication/unmet expectations/coaching opportunities.
  • 4. Mediate: coaching call, 1-week action plan with clear expectations, reassess at the end.
  • 5. Final decision: resolved → continue with support plan. Unresolved → change, with fault determining the new timeline.

Timeline by fault

  • HC fault (misconduct, severe misalignment, prolonged unresponsiveness): new start date 2 weeks out, matching begins immediately.
  • Intern fault: must book a call with PSA within 1 week or be withdrawn. Call clarifies requirements and expectations. Must then complete a reflective task — approval starts the 6-week placement process. Miss the task deadline → withdrawn.

RESTART vs RESUME

  • RESTART: full duration reset (e.g. 12-week internship → full 12 weeks again). New start/end dates. 16 weeks max.
  • RESUME: completed hours carry over, duration adjusted (e.g. 12-week with 5 done → 7 weeks at new placement). 16 weeks max.

Initiating an HC change (PSA steps)

  • Notify PMs/PRS/PAM in Slack immediately, with intern name, HC name, reason, HubSpot ticket link.
  • Mandatory call with the intern before actioning (skip only if HC is at fault) — confirm the change, outline next steps, answer questions.
  • Clarify with intern: reason, RESTART or RESUME preference, end-date adjustments, batch requirements (academic credit), profile updates.
  • Action in Moo: Manage Internships → search intern → View Internship → Change Company → choose Resume/Restart with reason. This terminates Internship 1 and sets the intern unplaced for Internship 2. Ensure the new start date is ≥2 weeks out for Resume. Update HubSpot notes, add HC Change Tag.
  • Confirm details with intern, schedule PRS call if CF change requested, announce in the HC-Changes-ISA-PRS-PMS Slack channel.
  • Credit-bearing programs (ASU, BAU): the platform auto-updates the end date — verify it aligns with program requirements, confirm hours/weeks needed for on-time graduation, add to notes and the Slack channel, and update the end date once the new placement is confirmed.

Important notes

  • PSAs/PMs must connect with host supervisors on LinkedIn at the start of every internship.
  • Before declaring anyone "unresponsive": at least 3 emails and 1 alternative channel (LinkedIn, WhatsApp, call) attempted.
  • Collaborate closely with PMS when an HC claims skill mismatch, when either side reports no relevant tasks, or when an intern was accepted despite a weaker profile.

Follow-up cadence (PSA-owned)

  • Weeks 1–2 after end date: 1 supportive reminder per party, Not_Started/In_Progress only. Keep tone supportive, reinforce value, consolidate intern+HC follow-ups into one email per recipient where relevant.

  • Always aim for one email per intern/HC even with multiple pending items.
  • Before any follow-up: confirm the assessment is open on platform, validate status in Metabase, cross-check off-platform submissions.
Escalation & mediation
Mediation Playbook Fast triage, the mediation spine, worked examples

A judgment framework, not a script library — two different PSAs following it properly should land on similar decisions even with different communication styles.

Core takeaways

  • Most conflict comes from expectation, communication, or skill gaps — not bad intent.
  • A structured process reduces emotional escalation and creates defensible decisions.
  • Fairness and clarity outweigh speed. Documented, consistent interventions prevent recurrence.

Sequence when a case lands

Pause (don't respond on instinct) → Triage the case → Run the mediation spine, every step → Choose the right intervention, not the fastest → Document continuously.

Fast triage — common case types

  • Performance concern — often hides unclear success criteria, skill–task mismatch, or thin feedback cadence.
  • Expectation mismatch — often hides weak onboarding, ambiguous scope, different seniority assumptions.
  • Communication breakdown — often hides async/sync norm mismatch, timezone friction, early-career habits.
  • Capacity/structural constraint — often hides internal HC workload issues, poor project planning, host over-commitment.
  • Values/fit tension — often hides stress or burnout, bias, mismatched working styles.

Don't lock into one category too early — many cases span more than one.

The mediation spine

  • 1. Gather information — acknowledge within 48h, calm/neutral/curious tone, speak to each side separately first. Never mediate on assumptions.
  • 2. Identify the core issue — list symptoms, map to skill/expectation/communication/structural causes, check for hidden causes (onboarding gaps, unclear benchmarks), validate against facts. Most poor outcomes trace to weak diagnosis, not bad intent.
  • 3. Confirm key facts — meeting timestamps, tasks assigned vs delivered, response times, feedback already shared. Screenshots help. Prevents story-vs-story deadlocks.
  • 4. Choose the right intervention — balance intern development, HC needs, and program integrity. Offer options not ultimatums, anchor in facts, set an observation window, document decisions.
  • 5. Communicate the decision — always include a 1–2 week observation window, clear actions for both sides, what success looks like, when follow-up happens. Summarise verbally, confirm in writing.
  • 6. Follow up and monitor — track patterns not single slips, progress against benchmarks, early repeat-issue signs. Not optional — this is where cases succeed or fail.

Intervention menu

  • Skill-related: SMART benchmarks, smaller milestones, learning resources/templates/coaching.
  • Expectation-related: reset scope and priorities, define "good" concretely, adjust timelines to match capacity.
  • Communication-related: daily/weekly check-ins, async status updates, explicit response-time norms.

Escalate when

Benchmarks are repeatedly missed · one party disengages · no willingness to adjust · program integrity or safety is at risk. Escalation is a decision, not a failure — don't let a case drag beyond 1–2 weeks. Options: final improvement window, reassignment, early closure.

When both sides are "right"

Reframe: what does the intern need to learn? What does the HC need to protect (time, quality, credibility)? What does the program need to uphold (fairness, consistency, outcomes)? If all three can't be satisfied simultaneously, program integrity outranks individual preference.

Worked examples

  • Performance vs expectation: HC says intern is slow → no onboarding docs, scope misunderstood → reset expectations + checklist + milestones → intern met deadlines, HC satisfied.
  • Communication breakdown: intern misses calls, HC frustrated → timezone misalignment + unclear async norms → weekly check-in schedule + Slack response norms → communication stabilized.
  • Capacity disguised as poor performance: HC says intern "does nothing" → HC overloaded, minimal tasks assigned → redistribute tasks + HC coaching → tasks completed, HC aware of responsibility.
  • Values/fit tension: tone complaints between HC and intern → cultural misunderstanding + early-career directness → 1:1 coaching on norms + reflection call → mutual understanding, de-escalated.
  • Repeated missed benchmarks: intern keeps missing milestones → lacks skills + unclear benchmarks → SMART goals + 1-week observation + escalation window → HC sees progress, structured escalation in place if pattern continues.
Decision-Making & Escalation Playbook When to own it vs when to escalate

If it's a risk, escalate. If it's a decision, own it. Not a policy or checklist — a shared operating framework for thinking under uncertainty. The goal isn't perfect decisions, it's momentum, clarity, and trust.

Our default expectation: ownership

Feeling unsure or stretched is normal — it isn't, on its own, a reason to escalate or defer. If progress can be made safely, it should be. Ownership means working through problems instead of pausing for guidance, using tools and precedent, and staying accountable for outcomes.

How to think before deciding

  • Identify the real problem: facts vs assumptions, actual blockers vs discomfort, temporary friction vs genuine risk. If nothing is truly blocked, it likely doesn't need escalation.
  • Ground yourself in existing tools: SOPs, the task bank, timeline levers (pauses, extensions, resequencing). These are starting points, not rigid rules — no perfect SOP fit is expected for every case.

Making the call

We optimize for progress over perfection. Own a decision when it's logical given available information, defensible if questioned later, and reversible if new information emerges. Confidence is built through practice, not pre-approval.

Communication is part of the decision

Any action should clearly state what's happening, what we're doing, and what happens next — to interns, HCs, and internal stakeholders alike.

Escalation is required for

Genuine compliance/university risk · legal exposure · safety concerns · reputational risk to the programme or company.

Escalation is not required for

Temporary delays (OOO, holidays) · resolvable ambiguity · anything a pause, task-bank assignment, or timeline adjustment can handle. These are exactly the scenarios where PSA judgment should be exercised.

How to escalate well

Good escalation accelerates progress, it doesn't pause it: clear context and background, what's already been tried, options considered, a recommended path forward. Work continues in parallel — escalation is not a hand-off of ownership.

Decision-making as a learning loop

Make the best call with available information → revisit when new information emerges → sense-check outcomes after action, not before. We'd rather calibrate decisions after progress than approve them before action. Mistakes handled thoughtfully are growth, not failure.

Common over-escalation patterns

  • Escalating discomfort, not risk — "I'm not 100% sure, so I wanted to check." Fix: make a reasonable call, document rationale, adjust if needed.
  • Escalating before using available tools — asking for guidance without checking SOPs or the task bank. Fix: use tools as a starting point even if the fit isn't perfect.
  • Treating escalation as a pause button — waiting to hear back instead of continuing the case. Fix: keep working in parallel, escalate with options and a recommendation.
  • Escalating resolvable ambiguity — asking others to interpret instead of clarifying. Fix: make a reasonable assumption, communicate it, set a check-in point.
  • Using escalation to transfer responsibility — handing off the decision entirely. Fix: escalate with context, options, and a recommendation — stay accountable.

What "good" looks like

  • Temporary delay: supervisor OOO 2 weeks, intern anxious → assign task-bank work, communicate clearly, keep monitoring without escalating.
  • Ambiguity: loosely defined scope → separate known from missing, use SOP intent/precedent for a reasonable assumption, propose a short-term plan with a check-in.
  • Post-decision calibration: paused an internship, new context emerges → explain original reasoning, share the update, adjust the plan — treat it as calibration, not error.

  • Requester closes the task once resolved.
  • Use HubSpot comments for context/questions instead of Slack or email — the intern record is the single source of truth.

Always verify the intern record before creating a task to avoid duplicates. Update tasks immediately as progress is made.

Product & Platform Knowledge How VI works, Moo basics, revenue model

Product

  • 18 Career Fields (CFs) — interns work 3 CF-related tasks + 2 cross-functional activities. Guaranteed first-choice CF, not guaranteed location or industry.
  • Linear interview process (one company at a time), with urgency-based exceptions near a start date.
  • Durations: 1/2/3/4 months at 10/20/30 hrs/week. Every intern gets 2 coaching calls, 24/7 CareerBridge, and monthly webinars regardless of track. All internships are unpaid — any payment is at the HC's discretion.
  • Revenue: ~90% B2B (universities — Sussex, ASU, Birmingham; pathway providers — Kaplan, Shorelight; MOOC/bootcamp — 2U-edX, IBT, Lighthouse; government-affiliated — Saudi Digital Academy, Misk), ~10% B2C direct signups. Host companies never pay — zero fees, zero salaries required.

HC eligibility criteria

  • Any industry except gambling/casinos, religious institutions, wines & spirits, tobacco.
  • Any geography except a defined restricted-country list (sanctions-driven).
  • Minimum 3 full-time employees, and a live website with a valid URL.
  • Supervisor must be full-time, intermediate+ English, register with a company-domain email. One persona can manage up to 3 interns at a time.
  • 1–2 employee companies: case-by-case approval by Head of Pre-Internship, considering hard-to-place fields and full-time contractor presence.

The "back-end" of the customer-facing platform. As a PSA you live mainly in "During Internship" and its subpages, occasionally in Registration and Matching. Access arrives via invitation early in week two — complete your profile with name, country, phone number, timezone.

How to structure your week

Daily reference

Your week has a rhythm — different days call for different priorities. This page walks through it day by day, then covers what to do when things pile up. For threshold definitions and SLA numbers, see Rules & Thresholds.

Always first — any day, before anything below
  • Safeguarding concerns
  • Non-attendance or complete communication breakdown
  • Any Critical status from a proactive call (Wk1 score 3, Wk3/Wk5 score 0–3)
  • Full Unreachable protocol triggered
  • Urgent tickets

When things pile up

These aren't fixed steps to run through in order — they're what tends to matter most, so you're not starting from zero when three things land on the same day. Real cases won't always fit neatly. Use your read of the actual situation over a static list — that's still the standard everywhere else on this site.

  • Always-first items (above) aren't a judgment call — they're a programme requirement, regardless of what else is happening that day.
  • Hard deadlines and Critical scores generally carry more weight than the rest, simply because they have the least flexibility — a Wednesday cutoff doesn't move, and Critical is same-day by definition.
  • At Risk scores, scheduled calls, and Supervisor Actions have a window rather than a single moment — spread them across the days available instead of leaving them all for the last one.
  • Bulk items and tickets are usually the most forgiving of the group, but that's a pattern, not a rule — if a ticket signals something that needs attention now, treat it like it does.

If something in front of you doesn't fit this pattern, trust your read of it. This page can't see the specific case — you can.

A few habits that help

  • Log as you go — catching up on old logs later usually means the day got away from you.
  • Front-load where you can — starting Wk1 calls Monday, even though the deadline is Wednesday, gives you room if someone's hard to reach.
  • Check last week first — a quick glance at last week's commitments before diving into a new one.

FAQ — "What Do I Do?"

The PSA team's practical guide to the questions and situations that come up during an internship. Use this when you're thinking "something has happened — what do I do now?"

This isn't a replacement for the wiki or the formal SOPs. Where a process or SOP exists, that remains the source of truth. This page gives you the context and judgement around a situation so you know when and why to use those processes.

Getting started
The PSA mindset What you're actually responsible for, who you support

What is the PSA actually responsible for? Not just answering questions, chasing forms, or completing calls. You're responsible for understanding how the internship is actually going, spotting problems early, and stepping in when something could affect the experience. The question to keep coming back to: "Is this internship moving in the right direction?" — not "have I completed everything I'm supposed to complete?" A placement can be technically up to date while the intern is having a poor experience, and vice versa.

Who is the PSA supporting — intern or Host Company? Both. Their interests genuinely conflict sometimes. Don't automatically side with whoever contacted you first: understand both perspectives, establish the facts, understand what each person expected, check the relevant requirements, look at the impact, then decide what needs to change.

How do I know whether something is actually a problem? Three things — Pattern (one-off or repeating?), Impact (is it actually affecting the experience or requirements?), Trajectory (better, same, or worse?). A missed email is different from repeated non-response. Low hours in Week 1 is different from low hours in Week 7 with no recovery plan.

What's the biggest judgement mistake a new PSA can make? Assuming the first version of the story is the whole story. An intern's "my supervisor doesn't support me" might be genuine — or it might be that they're missing scheduled check-ins, or the supervisor is supporting them in a way they don't recognise. Don't rush to decide who's right — find out what's actually happening.

Should I wait for all the facts before contacting the other party? No. You can start building the Host Company relationship while still investigating an intern's concern, without saying "the intern has complained about you." A routine check-in gives you another perspective and makes a later difficult conversation easier. Don't wait until the relationship is already damaged.

I'm a new PSA First assignment, intro email, first call, shadowing

What should I do when an internship is assigned to me? Get the basic picture first — intern details, Host Company, supervisor, start date, duration, programme type, academic-credit requirements, existing communication and concerns. Don't rush into contacting someone based on incomplete information.

When should I send the introduction email? Once the internship is assigned and visible to you. If someone tells you that you have an intern but you can't see the placement in Moo, double-check the assignment first — don't assume the system is wrong.

When do I get to do my first call? Once you've completed onboarding and your first placements are assigned. Week 1 calls are completed and logged through HubSpot.

Can I shadow another PSA's call? Not routinely — calls are handled through HubSpot, so live shadowing can be difficult. Where possible, the team can add you to booked meetings so you can observe. But don't wait forever for the perfect shadowing opportunity — learn the process, understand the structure, and start taking calls yourself.

Week 1 & the first conversation Purpose of the call, common questions, no-answer scenarios

What's the purpose of the Week 1 call? Activation and relationship-building. Understand: has the internship started, does the intern know their supervisor, have they been contacted, do they have a schedule and work, do they understand expectations, do they know what the PSA does and when to reach you, is anything already wrong. But don't turn it into a checklist — you're also establishing the relationship that makes future intervention easier. If the first time an intern hears from you is when something's gone wrong, it's much harder to have a productive conversation.

What questions do interns most likely ask in Week 1? HC expectations ("what should I be doing in my first week?"), communication ("how often should I talk to my supervisor?"), hours/scheduling ("how do I track my hours?"), requirements ("what forms do I need?"), problems ("what if I'm not enjoying it?"), career development ("can this lead to a job?"), and platform/admin questions. If you don't know an answer, say so and find the right one — don't guess.

What if the intern doesn't answer my call but responds by email? Use your judgement. If the email establishes they've started, know their supervisor, have a schedule and tasks, and the internship appears healthy, you don't need to force a phone call just to say it happened. Compare "sorry, was in class, started Monday, meeting twice a week, already got my first project" against no answer + no email response + supervisor hasn't heard from them + missing WFL. The first points to a healthy start; the second is multiple signals of a possible engagement problem. Assess the internship, not just whether the call happened.

What if the intern has started but has no tasks? Address it — an internship can't be meaningful without meaningful work. Find out why: supervisor hasn't had time, intern waiting for access, supervisor unclear what to assign, genuinely no work, or a communication problem. Don't dismiss it because the supervisor is friendly — the issue is whether the internship provides meaningful work, not whether the supervisor is nice. Start the intern on CareerBridge while you support contacting the company.

The Touchpoint Model Why it exists, healthy placements, mid-touchpoint problems

Why do we have the Touchpoint Model? A structured, proactive way to stay connected throughout the internship — finding problems earlier rather than waiting for a complaint, an escalation, a Critical WFL, or a placement that's become difficult to recover. A touchpoint isn't just another task to complete — it's an opportunity to find out what's actually happening.

What happens during the different touchpoints? Follow the current Touchpoint Model/SOP for exact requirements — broadly Week 1 Activation, Week 2 WFL Triage, Week 3 Trajectory Check, Week 4 WFL Triage, Week 5 Finish Strong/Midpoint Course-Correct. The important part isn't memorising the headings — every touchpoint should help you understand the health and trajectory of the internship. Detailed execution sits in the Touchpoint Model/SOP; this FAQ doesn't duplicate that.

What if everything looks healthy? Complete the required touchpoint anyway. Healthy placements still need contact — it keeps the relationship active and makes it much easier to step in if something changes later.

What if I discover a problem during a touchpoint? Don't wait for the next one. Move into Investigate → Coach/Intervene → Monitor → Escalate if required, right away. If a Week 3 conversation reveals no meaningful work for two weeks, deal with it now, not at Week 4.

What if the placement is already at risk before the scheduled touchpoint? Act when you see the risk. The schedule makes proactive engagement consistent — it should never stop you from responding earlier.

WFL & risk signals
Weekly Feedback — WFL Score bands, discrepancies between sides, no explanation given

WFL = Weekly Feedback. Each week, interns and supervisors rate their experience 1–10.

ScoreWhat it tells you
8–10Healthy
6–7Something may need attention
1–5Critical / action required

The score is a signal. Your job is to understand the signal.

What if an intern gives a WFL of 5? Act on it — it's Critical and needs prompt follow-up. Don't let the rest of the dashboard talk you out of it. Healthy hours, healthy submissions, and a responsive supervisor don't make a 5 less important — they mean there's something about the experience the other metrics aren't showing you. Ask "why is this intern telling us the experience isn't fine?", not "everything else looks fine, I'll deal with this Thursday."

What if the WFL is 6 or 7? Investigate. Not necessarily a crisis, but a signal something may need attention. What's driving the score? What changed? Is this isolated? Is the other party aware? Can it be fixed easily? Is the trajectory improving or worsening?

What if the intern and supervisor give very different scores? Don't choose a side. An intern rating "my supervisor doesn't give me enough support" 4/10 against a supervisor's "everything is going really well" 9/10 — the difference itself is the interesting part. What does "support" mean to the intern? What has the supervisor actually provided? How often do they meet, and is the intern attending? The discrepancy is useful information on its own.

What if the WFL is low but there's no explanation? Follow up. Don't write off the score because the comment box is empty — the score is telling you something, and your conversation should help you understand what.

What if the low WFL seems outside the Host Company's control? Still investigate. The point isn't finding someone to blame — the intern could be unhappy because of university requirements, scheduling, a technical issue, a misunderstanding, their own expectations, or a personal circumstance. Understand the cause before deciding what to do.

Intern engagement Missed calls, unreachable, withdrawal requests

What if an intern doesn't answer my calls? Don't immediately label them disengaged. Have they responded another way? Are they completing required activities? Communicating with the supervisor? Is it a timezone issue, a one-off, or becoming a pattern? One missed call + email response + healthy internship is very different from repeated missed calls + no email response + missed WFL + supervisor also can't reach them.

What if an intern becomes completely unreachable? Document what you've tried — last successful contact, email/call/WhatsApp attempts, HC contact, existing risk indicators — then follow the relevant unresponsive/withdrawal process. Don't keep treating it as just a missed call.

What if the intern wants to withdraw? First understand why — what happened, is the issue fixable, have they discussed it with the HC, is it the work, the supervisor, hours, university requirements, or something else. Then follow the Internship Change/Withdrawal SOP.

Does the Partner Requirements Cheat Sheet decide whether a withdrawal is possible? No — it tells you the partner's default position, not the final decision. For most partners, the default under "Can Interns Withdraw Unilaterally?" is No, route: Partner must approve, notify PAM. A few follow the SLA process instead. That default tells you PAM needs to be involved early, but it can't capture how far into the internship the intern is, whether the issue is fixable, whether the partner has flexed before, or what the university allows. A "No" on the sheet is where the conversation with PAM begins, not necessarily where it ends.

What should I do if an intern wants to withdraw? Investigate first, work out if it's fixable, check the partner's row in the cheat sheet, check financial implications (e.g. University of Hertfordshire charges 50% pre-acceptance / 100% post-acceptance; Arizona State has a fee at any stage), loop in PAM early. If there's a genuine retention chance, say so explicitly. Don't imply withdrawal is automatically simple or cost-free. If the intern is genuinely set on leaving and the issue isn't fixable, don't push retention for its own sake — give PAM the facts.

What if the intern wants to withdraw and then becomes unreachable? Document the original request — don't treat the case as though they never contacted you. Document follow-up attempts, then follow the applicable process. Current unresponsive process: 2 calls + 1 voicemail + email + text over 7 days. If unsure whether a withdrawal can be processed without further confirmation, check the SOP rather than guessing.

Risk priority When a healthy dashboard doesn't mean a healthy experience

Hours and submissions are healthy but the WFL is 5 — what now? Critical. Don't average everything together and decide "most things look good" — the WFL is a direct experience signal.

What if the issue is "Slipping" or "Disengaged"? These are engagement/trajectory signals shown in the Queue. Don't automatically treat them as SLA — use the relevant priority framework and investigate the cause.

What if the dashboard looks healthy but the intern says the internship is poor? Investigate. The dashboard doesn't replace the intern's experience — it tells you what's happening operationally, the conversation tells you what it actually feels like. Both matter. Don't say "but everything looks healthy" to an intern telling you they're not learning anything.

Hours, academic requirements & CareerBridge
Hours & scheduling Flexing requirements, low hours, HC pushing beyond approved limits

Do partner hour requirements ever flex? Check the cheat sheet first, but if changing hours could keep an intern on the programme instead of withdrawing, ask PAM — don't automatically assume no. Some requirements are contractual/preference-based (PAM may be able to negotiate), others are legally mandated (e.g. University of Hertfordshire's 15-hour weekly limit is tied to UKVI legislation — PAM can't negotiate that away, but there may be other ways to keep the intern on the programme). Ask what's possible before deciding there's no option.

What if an intern has logged fewer hours than required? Look at the stage and the reason. Lower hours early on don't automatically mean something's wrong — a Week 1 intern with fewer hours but a started internship, engaged supervisor, tasks, schedule, and positive feedback is a monitoring/coaching situation, not a crisis, if there's time left to recover.

What if the internship isn't for academic credit? Still check the hours, but don't automatically treat a shortfall as Critical. "12 hours instead of 15, but we've agreed I'll make it up next week" is very different from "only 5 hours because my supervisor doesn't have anything for me" — the second may actually be a Host Company quality problem.

What if an academic-credit internship is struggling with hours? Take it seriously — don't wait until Week 7 if a Week 3 intern in an 8-week internship already has a significant shortfall. Work out required vs. completed hours, why the gap exists, whether the HC understands the requirement, whether the schedule can adjust, whether the remaining hours can realistically be recovered, and any university/programme constraints. Find the problem while there's still something you can do about it.

What if the Host Company expects more hours than the intern is supposed to work? No — don't agree to it, and don't treat it as a judgement call. The intern's hours need to match what's contracted or approved. This is different from asking PAM whether an adjustment could help retain an intern — a HC simply asking an intern to exceed an approved limit is not something to agree to. Explain the requirement; escalate if they push back.

What if the intern wants to change their working hours? Understand why first, then check whether the proposed schedule still meets requirements and partner rules. Don't promise the intern it's possible. If the change could prevent a withdrawal, raise it with PAM.

Academic-credit vs. skills/career interns Different motivations, same problem can land differently

Academic-credit interns often care more about requirements, deadlines, forms, evaluations, hours, and university expectations. Skills/career-focused interns often care more about meaningful projects, learning, mentorship, networking, and future employment. Neither matters more — but understanding the motivation helps you understand what risk looks like for that specific intern.

A good early question: "At the end of this internship, what would make you feel it was successful?" You might hear "I need to earn credit," "I want to learn project management," "I want something strong for my CV," or "I want a job offer" — each tells you what matters to that intern.

Why this matters: the same slow-feedback supervisor might worry an academic-credit intern about their evaluation completing, while a career-focused intern is worried about not getting enough feedback to improve. Same Host Company issue, different support needed.

CareerBridge & programme requirements Is it mandatory, explaining requirements to interns

Is CareerBridge mandatory for every intern? No — mandatory for certain interns completing an internship for academic credit. Check the Partner Requirements Cheat Sheet and relevant programme guidance for the specific placement.

What if an intern asks why they have to complete a requirement? Explain the reason where you can — don't just say "because the system says so." If academic, explain there are requirements attached beyond the day-to-day work with the Host Company. If you don't know the exact reason, check — don't guess.

Host Company management
Host Company engagement Missed WFLs, chasing without annoying, no tasks assigned

What if a supervisor doesn't complete a WFL? Start with a reminder, then look at the bigger picture — is this the first missed one, have they completed previous ones, are they otherwise responsive and assigning work, is the intern having a healthy experience, is this becoming a pattern. A single missed WFL and a pattern of non-response are not the same thing.

How do I chase a supervisor without annoying them? This is a big part of PSA judgement. The goal isn't "get the WFL completed at any cost" — it's "keep the relationship productive while still holding them accountable." Progression: Reminder → Follow-up → Change approach/channel → Investigate the broader pattern → Escalate if needed. Don't send five versions of the same email if the real problem is disengagement — call them, text them, connect on LinkedIn, explain why it matters, ask if there's a practical barrier.

What if the supervisor doesn't assign tasks? Treat this as more serious than a missing WFL — a missing WFL is administrative, no meaningful work is an internship-quality problem. A supervisor who's missed one WFL but the intern has regular meetings, meaningful work, and a good relationship needs just a reminder. Multiple missed WFLs + no email response + no meaningful work + intern waiting is several signals pointing the same direction — that needs stronger intervention.

What if the supervisor is responsive but provides poor support? Being responsive isn't enough — look at whether the intern is getting meaningful work, guidance, feedback, learning, room to ask questions, and reasonable autonomy. A supervisor can answer every email and still provide a poor internship experience.

What if the Host Company says "I've worked with VI for years"? Don't become defensive, but don't let their history override current requirements either. VI's processes change and a long-standing HC may genuinely be surprised by a new one. Acknowledge the history, explain the current requirement, understand their concern, find the most practical way forward.

What if the supervisor is new and overwhelmed by WFLs and assessments? Coach first — they may simply not understand the process yet. Make the requirement easy to understand and action. Don't treat someone as an unreliable Host Company for missing one requirement while still learning.

SLA & reliability When SLA actually triggers, SLA 1 vs SLA 2

Does the SLA process trigger because a PSA misses a Week 2/4/6 call? No. This distinction matters — the trigger isn't "we skipped the call," it's WFL score ≤7 → PSA follow-up → intern/supervisor doesn't respond → review against the Reliability & SLA Tagging Framework. A PSA failing their own follow-up is an internal operational issue, not automatically an SLA trigger for the Host Company.

When should I apply an SLA tag? Don't apply one just because someone was slow once — use the framework and the evidence. Flow: Identify the issue → Confirm the evidence → Apply the tag in Moo with rationale → Communicate where required → Reassess. Evidence: dates, communication attempts, WFLs, evaluations, emails, texts, impact on the internship.

What is SLA 1? Moderate Risk — one late onboarding, WFL completion 51–65%, minor inconsistency. Response is monitoring, not an immediate placement restriction.

What is SLA 2? High Risk — recurring/unresponsive behaviour, >50% WFLs missed, no work reported. Consequences: placement pause, mandatory intervention call before a new intern is assigned, blacklist applied before that call.

What if a Host Company has been unresponsive for 1.5 weeks but the intern says everything's fine? Don't turn time into an automatic SLA rule. Non-response matters, but so does the intern actively communicating with the supervisor, having work, a clear schedule, and saying it's going well. Look at the actual framework criteria and document the evidence rather than defaulting to "1.5 weeks = SLA 2."

What if the Host Company is repeatedly unresponsive and there's no work? Now you have a much stronger reliability concern — multiple missed WFLs, repeated non-response, no meaningful work, intern affected, previous intervention hasn't worked. May meet criteria for SLA 2 or blacklisting. Use the framework, not instinct alone.

What happens to an SLA tag over time? Track: Reliable → SLA 1 – Moderate Risk → SLA 2 – High Risk → Previously Blacklisted. Host Companies need three consecutive clean placements to earn back Reliable status.

Host Company blacklisting Evidence-based only, difficult ≠ bad

How does blacklisting work? Use the Host Company Blacklist Process and the Reliability & SLA Tagging Framework together — evidence-based, following the established process.

Should I blacklist a Host Company because I have a bad feeling about them? No. Your instinct should tell you to investigate — it shouldn't replace the evidence.

What if the Host Company is difficult but provides an excellent internship? "Difficult to deal with" doesn't automatically mean "bad Host Company." A demanding, blunt supervisor who isn't enthusiastic about VI admin but gives meaningful work, meets regularly, gives feedback, and supports learning may need careful relationship management — that doesn't make them unsuitable. Don't confuse personality with risk.

Conflict, withdrawals & change
Intern vs. Host Company conflict Establishing facts before deciding who's right

What if the intern and Host Company genuinely disagree? Don't start with "who's right?" — start with "what does each person believe should be happening?" Then establish expectations, requirements, communication, evidence, and impact.

Scenario — academic requirements vs. workload: intern needs required hours, HC doesn't think they need them working that much. Don't automatically side with either — establish the actual academic/programme requirement and whether the HC's expectation conflicts with it, then work out a solution or escalate.

Scenario — support dispute: intern says "my supervisor never meets with me," supervisor says "I have meetings scheduled weekly, they don't attend." Don't decide who's telling the truth immediately — check meeting invites, attendance, communication, expectations, and whether the meetings are actually useful. The goal is fixing the underlying issue, not proving someone wrong.

Withdrawals & Host Company changes When a change is warranted, cheat sheet defaults

Can a PSA request a Host Company change? Only within what the partner allows — but don't treat a "No" or "Not Advised" in the cheat sheet as the end of the conversation. Check the partner's default under "Can Host Company Be Changed?": Not Advised (common with academic-credit partners, since a change can affect timeline, graduation, or visa status), Case by Case (no strong default), or Yes – Operational Need (some non-credit partners allow it). Then look at the actual situation.

What should I do before asking for a Host Company change? Establish whether the problem is actually fixable — what's wrong, is it temporary, what's already been tried, has the HC had a chance to improve, is meaningful work still happening, is trust fundamentally broken, are there programme/university restrictions, would a change actually solve it. A difficult supervisor isn't automatically a reason to change.

What if an HC change could prevent a withdrawal? Raise it with PAM explicitly if it looks like the difference between keeping the intern and losing them. Don't rule it out yourself because the cheat sheet says "Not Advised" — and don't promise the intern it'll happen either. PAM owns the partner conversation. Follow the Internship Change Guidelines and use the relevant #hc-changes-psa-prs-pm channel.

When should I recommend a Host Company change? Not simply because the intern doesn't like the supervisor. "My supervisor is strict and I don't like their communication style" alone probably isn't enough. "I've raised this three times, I'm not getting meaningful work, my supervisor won't communicate, we've tried to fix it and nothing's changed" means you're looking at whether the placement is still viable.

What if the intern simply doesn't like the Host Company? Don't automatically process a change — find out what "I don't like it" actually means. Could be the work isn't what they expected, not enough work, a difficult supervisor, industry dislike, unrealistic expectations, or a genuine quality issue. The response depends on the reason.

Serious issues & decision-making
Serious & sensitive issues What to escalate immediately, incomplete facts

What should I escalate immediately? Don't treat these like normal internship problems: harassment allegations, discrimination, bullying, misconduct, inappropriate behaviour, threats to safety/wellbeing/security, labour regulation violations, university requirement violations, immediate HC termination requests, intern resignation/abandonment without notice, legal or regulatory complaints, media complaints, significant trust breakdowns, NDA/contracting concerns. When in doubt, ask for support.

What if I don't have all the facts? If the potential risk is high, escalate anyway. Escalating doesn't mean "I've decided what happened" — it means "something potentially serious has been raised and I want the right people to have visibility while I establish the facts." You can investigate while it's escalated. Don't wait until you've proven everything.

What if an intern says they don't feel safe? Treat it as high-risk. Escalate immediately through the appropriate process. Don't try to independently resolve a safety or safeguarding concern.

What if there's an NDA or contract issue? If you're not completely comfortable handling it, come to the PSA Lead/team for support. Don't improvise contractual advice — these situations can create significant downstream issues if handled incorrectly.

The PSA Decision Framework The 12-step process for when you genuinely don't know what to do
  1. What actually happened? Separate facts from assumptions.
  2. Who is affected? The intern? The Host Company? Both? The university/programme? VI?
  3. Is anyone at immediate risk? If yes — escalate.
  4. Is there a Critical signal? If yes — act promptly.
  5. Is this a one-off or a pattern? Look at the history.
  6. What evidence do I have? WFLs, hours, assessments, HubSpot, Moo, emails, WhatsApp, meetings, intern feedback, HC feedback, partner requirements.
  7. Have I heard both sides? If there's disagreement, don't decide based on one account unless the risk requires immediate action.
  8. Can coaching solve it? If yes — coach.
  9. Does it need intervention? If yes — intervene and monitor.
  10. Does an SOP apply? If yes — follow the SOP. This FAQ gives you the judgement around the situation; the SOP gives you the detailed process.
  11. Does another team own the decision? If yes — route it to the right team while keeping visibility.
  12. Have I documented it? If it materially affects the internship — log it.
When in doubt The do-nothing test

If you're still unsure, ask yourself: "If I do nothing, what happens next?"

  • "Probably nothing." → Monitor.
  • "The issue could become harder to fix." → Intervene.
  • "Someone could be harmed, the internship could break down, or there could be legal/programme implications." → Escalate.

You don't need to have every answer. You do need to recognise when something matters.

The PSA Principles Ten operating principles, including "everything is a conversation"
  • Don't assume. Investigate. The first version of the story isn't always the whole story.
  • Don't wait. Intervene early. A problem is usually easier to fix before it becomes a pattern.
  • Don't overreact. Look at the evidence. Not every difficult situation needs escalation.
  • Don't ignore patterns. One small issue may be manageable. Repeated small issues can become serious.
  • Don't take sides. Understand both perspectives.
  • Don't let systems replace judgement. Moo, HubSpot, WFLs, and the Queue give you information — they don't make the decision for you.
  • Don't let relationships replace accountability. Being supportive of a Host Company doesn't mean letting requirements slide.
  • Don't let policy replace humanity. The goal isn't simply compliance — it's a successful internship experience.
  • Everything is a conversation if you start it. A partner default of "No" or "Not Advised" is a starting position, not necessarily the final answer. If there's a realistic way to keep an intern on the programme, raise it with PAM — don't let a spreadsheet column decide before the conversation happens.

And finally: the PSA's job isn't to have every answer. It's to know how to find the right answer, recognise when something matters, ask the right questions, look at the full picture, and act before a manageable problem becomes an unmanageable one. Good PSA work isn't about reacting to everything — it's about knowing what matters, why it matters, and what to do next.

Communication & systems
Communication WhatsApp, logging outside HubSpot, call vs. email

Can I use WhatsApp for work? Yes — commonly used. You can use a work number and add it to your email signature. WhatsApp Business is optional.

Should everything happen through WhatsApp? No — use the channel that fits the situation. For important internship communication, make sure the outcome is captured in HubSpot even if the conversation happened on WhatsApp.

What if an important conversation happened outside HubSpot? Log it — including WhatsApp, phone calls, external meetings, or relevant Slack. If it materially affects the internship, the outcome needs to be captured.

When should I call rather than email? Use judgement — consider urgency, sensitivity, complexity, and whether written communication has worked. If you've sent three emails and you're having the same conversation over and over, change the approach. A call can resolve in ten minutes what email won't resolve in three days.

Moo vs. HubSpot System of record vs. system of engagement

Moo = System of Record. Core data — contact details, company details, internship/placement records, website URLs, structural placement information. Moo is the primary source of truth for these; sync runs Moo → HubSpot. If you change a synced field directly in HubSpot, the change may be overwritten or may not persist.

HubSpot = System of Engagement. Emails, calls, meetings, HelpDesk, tasks, escalations, communication history, operational workflows, placement support activity.

What if I need to change a contact's email address? Don't change it directly in HubSpot. Contact email changes go through #epd-please-help to EPD, after which the change syncs to HubSpot.

HubSpot, meetings & tasks Meeting links, when to create a task, what to write in a note

Where do I find my meeting link? In HubSpot — use your own link in communication where appropriate.

When should I create a HubSpot task? When there's a specific follow-up action that needs to happen — "follow up with supervisor on Friday if no WFL response" is a task. A note tells you what happened; a task tells you what needs to happen next. For task priority, naming, and SLA detail, use the HubSpot Tasks for Internship Support guidance.

What should I document in a HubSpot note? What happened, when, who was involved, what was said/done, what's the impact, what happens next. "Spoke to intern, all good" isn't useful six weeks later — capture the context someone would actually need.

Technical issues Don't guess, don't invent a workaround

What if an intern reports a technical issue? First work out what's actually broken — what are they trying to do, what happens when they try, is there an error message, is it affecting their ability to complete the internship, is it actually a platform issue or a process misunderstanding. Then use the appropriate technical support route. Don't invent a workaround when you don't know whether it's safe or correct.

What if I don't know the answer? Ask. Don't say "I think this should work..." when you don't know — say "let me check this and come back to you," then find the answer through the appropriate resource. "I don't know" is fine. Guessing isn't.

Common mistakes & case studies
Common PSA mistakes Five recurring patterns worth watching for in yourself

Biggest technical mistake: not documenting communication that happens outside HubSpot — WhatsApp, phone calls, external meetings, important Slack. If it materially affects the internship, capture the outcome.

Biggest communication mistake: assuming the first complaint is the full story. Investigate.

Another common mistake: waiting until a problem is completely confirmed before contacting the other party. Maintaining contact with the Host Company while investigating an intern's concern gives you more context and makes intervention easier later.

Biggest risk-management mistake: treating a serious issue like a normal internship problem. Legal, safeguarding, safety, misconduct, contractual, or serious relationship concerns — escalate early.

Biggest Host Company relationship mistake: being so worried about annoying a supervisor that you stop holding them accountable. Relationship management and accountability aren't opposites — a good PSA can be warm + clear + persistent.

Biggest intern-support mistake: trying to solve every problem for the intern. Support and coach them while helping them develop their own professional judgement — sometimes the best support isn't doing something for them, it's helping them work out how to handle it themselves.

Case studies Fifteen worked scenarios, from a silent supervisor to a happy supervisor with an unhappy intern

1. The Silent Supervisor

Two missed WFLs, two unanswered emails — but the intern says everything's fine, they talk daily, and they're getting lots of work. Don't jump straight to SLA 2. Non-response matters, but so does the intern's actual experience — look at how many WFLs are missing, how many attempts you've made, whether there's other HC communication, and whether the situation actually meets the SLA criteria. Lesson: non-response matters, context matters too.

2. The Perfect Dashboard

Healthy hours, healthy submissions, no missed requirements, positive supervisor feedback — but the intern gives a WFL of 5 and says they're miserable and not learning anything. Contact them promptly; the WFL is Critical. Then find out why the experience and the metrics don't match. Lesson: a healthy dashboard doesn't cancel a poor experience.

3. The Angry Intern

"My supervisor is terrible, they don't support me at all." Don't immediately email the HC with the complaint — first understand what happened, what "support" means to the intern, how often they communicate, what work and feedback they're receiving, whether meetings are scheduled and attended. Lesson: take the complaint seriously without treating the first version as the final version.

4. The Happy Supervisor

Supervisor rates the intern 9/10 — "they're doing great." Intern rates the experience 4/10 — "I'm learning nothing." Investigate the difference: the intern may be performing well but still feel underutilised, and the supervisor may be happy with performance while not providing enough development. Lesson: good performance and good experience aren't necessarily the same thing.

5. Week 1 Low Hours

Fewer hours than expected in Week 1, but the intern's started successfully, the supervisor's engaged, tasks are assigned, there's a clear schedule, feedback is positive. Understand the reason and monitor — don't automatically escalate. Lesson: timing matters.

6. Week 3 Academic-Credit Intern

Significantly behind on hours by Week 3 of an 8-week internship; the HC says "we don't really need them for more hours." Act now — establish required vs. completed hours, why the gap exists, whether the HC understands the requirement, and whether the shortfall can realistically be recovered within the applicable requirements. Lesson: a recoverable problem in Week 3 can become an impossible problem in Week 7.

7. The Host Company Wants Out

"This isn't working. We want to terminate the intern immediately." Don't treat this as a normal conversation — immediate termination requests need investigation and resolution attempts. Gather the facts, document what's happened, involve the appropriate teams. Lesson: don't jump straight to termination.

8. The Intern Wants to Withdraw

"I want to withdraw, I hate this internship." Don't immediately process it — ask what they mean: the work, the supervisor, the hours, the company, expectations, something academic, something else. Then work out if it's fixable, and if the intern's open to staying, check partner requirements and raise retention with PAM. Lesson: "I want to withdraw" is an outcome — find the reason before deciding what happens next.

9. The Intern Wants to Withdraw — Then Disappears

They request withdrawal, you respond, they stop replying. Document the original request and your follow-up attempts, let PAM know, then follow the usual process — if unresponsive with no PAM feedback, withdraw. Don't chase them indefinitely as if they never told you what they wanted. Lesson: the original request is part of the case history.

10. The Difficult Supervisor

Blunt and demanding, the intern doesn't particularly like them — but they meet regularly, provide meaningful work and feedback, and the intern is learning with healthy WFLs. Not grounds for a Host Company change based on personality alone; work with the supervisor on better mentorship too — coaching isn't just for the intern. Lesson: don't confuse discomfort with risk.

11. The Nice Supervisor With No Work

Lovely, responsive, well-liked by the intern — but almost nothing to do. Not healthy, but not an immediate HC change either. Help the supervisor craft meaningful work using the original offer first; change only if that's not feasible. Lesson: a good relationship doesn't compensate for a lack of meaningful work.

12. The Long-Term Host Company

"We've worked with VI for five years, we've never had to do this before." Don't become defensive, but don't abandon the current requirement — explain the process, understand their concern, find the most practical way forward. Lesson: respect the relationship without letting history override current requirements.

13. The Technical Issue

"I can't access the platform" and you don't know why. Don't guess — find out what they're trying to access, what happens, whether there's an error, whether it's account-specific, then route it through the established support channel. Lesson: "let me check with our team" is acceptable, inventing an answer isn't.

14. The WFL 5 With Everything Else Green

WFL 5, hours healthy, submissions healthy, supervisor responsive. Critical — prompt contact, don't let the other metrics dilute the signal. Lesson: the score is the priority signal.

15. The Supervisor Who Won't Respond

Missed several WFLs, hasn't responded to multiple follow-ups, hasn't assigned meaningful work — and the intern is now concerned. This isn't just "please complete your WFL" anymore — document the evidence, assess against the SLA criteria, escalate through the appropriate framework. Lesson: when administrative non-compliance starts affecting the internship, the issue has changed.

Archetypes in Action: Jason Supervisor styles — "Ones" vs "Twos"

From the shared pre-reading, "Supervisor Archetypes: A Guide for ISAs" — recurring leadership patterns Ben Horowitz calls "Ones" and "Twos."

The Ones — strategic decision-makers

Thrive on big ideas, rapid decisions, incomplete information. Gather insight from many sources and turn it into decisive action.

  • Strengths: see the chessboard, plot moves ahead, keep momentum alive.
  • Blind spots: lose patience with execution detail — process design, training, accountability systems.
  • Impact on interns: freedom, creativity, exposure to strategy — but risk of confusion, inconsistent feedback. Unless the intern is a self-starter, working under a One can feel like being dropped in the deep end.

The Twos — operational executors

Thrive on clarity, order, discipline. Love structured plans, clear timelines, stable processes.

  • Strengths: execution, accountability, scaling reliable systems — nothing falls through the cracks.
  • Blind spots: undervalue unstructured thinking time; ambiguity feels like risk, not opportunity.
  • Impact on interns: clear expectations, predictability, strong guidance — but work can feel repetitive, limited room for exploration. Great for detail-oriented interns, can box in creative ones.

Flexibility & overlap

Most supervisors aren't pure Ones or Twos — many flex depending on context (a One in strategy meetings, a Two running weekly reviews). The goal isn't to label a supervisor, but to identify their default gear, since that shapes what the intern experiences.

Why this matters

  • With a One, interns often say "I don't know what's expected of me."
  • With a Two, interns often say "I don't have space to be creative."

Misdiagnosing the pattern means blaming the wrong thing — "the intern isn't proactive" often really means the supervisor is a One who hasn't given enough guardrails. "The supervisor is too rigid" often means a Two who values process over improvisation. Reframing the problem as style alignment shifts you from firefighting to proactive translation.

Caution: avoid boxing supervisors in

This is a lens, not a label. Don't tell a supervisor "you're a One" — that feels reductive. Use it as a working hypothesis: "I notice they move fast on ideas but get less detailed on process — that's where interns may struggle." Spot the default gear, not the whole engine.

ISA role

  • With Ones: add structure, clarity, and checkpoints to help interns feel anchored.
  • With Twos: create room for creativity, flexibility, and growth so interns don't feel stifled.

Human-Centred Communication The purpose / perception / polish framework

Session purpose: strengthen the team's ability to write human, purposeful, efficient emails — replace formulaic templates with empathetic, audience-aware communication. Move from transactional to relational interactions.

Emails are often the only point of connection with interns and HCs — they define how people feel about working with us. Interns want to feel guided, supported, encouraged. Supervisors want clarity and brevity. Tone is strategy: how we say things shapes whether people want to keep engaging.

From template to tailored — a worked example

Cold template: "Dear [Name], This is a reminder to complete the weekly feedback form for your intern. Please submit it by Friday. Failure to submit may have consequences."

Tailored for an intern: "Hi [Name], Hope your week's gone well! Just a reminder to complete your reflection form before Friday — it helps us track your progress and share feedback with your supervisor. I'm excited to see your thoughts on what's gone well and what could improve."

Tailored for a supervisor: "Hi [Name], Hope your week's going smoothly. Just a quick note that the weekly feedback form for [Intern Name] is due by Friday — it only takes 2–3 minutes to complete and keeps us aligned on progress. Appreciate your time as always."

Tailored ≠ verbose — empathy can be efficient.

Three filters before sending

  • Purpose — what outcome am I trying to achieve? Is this the clearest way to get it?
  • Perception — how will this feel to the recipient? Supportive? Demanding? Respectful?
  • Polish — is it clean, skimmable, and in line with our tone of voice?

Tone shifts between interns and supervisors, but the underlying principles — intentional, empathetic, clear — don't. Each message either earns trust or erodes it.

Supportive Accountability with Interns Safety Net vs. Crutch — the four accountability moves

The trade-off we feel: holding the standard means losing the warmth, keeping the warmth means softening the standard. So we trade, and wonder why neither side feels supported. The reframe — what if it's a false choice? Accountability, done well, is support. Choosing not to hold an intern accountable isn't kindness; it's a quiet vote of no confidence in their ability to handle the truth. Holding the standard says: I believe you can rise to this.

The metaphor — Safety Net vs. Crutch

A safety net catches you when you fall. It does not carry you. A crutch does the walking for you — when we walk interns through every step, we aren't supporting their growth, we're doing it for them. Mid-email or mid-call, ask yourself: am I being the net right now, or am I being the crutch?

AreaCrutchSafety net
CommitmentsExcusing a missed commitment so the intern doesn't feel badNaming the missed commitment plainly, asking the intern to account for it
CommunicationRelaying the intern's excuses to the HC on their behalfCoaching the intern to speak to the HC themselves; stepping in only to translate
OwnershipProposing the catch-up plan yourself so the intern can just agreeAsking the intern to propose the plan, then refining it together

The foundation — pause before you move

Notice whose discomfort you're soothing. The instinct to over-help often protects you from the friction of a hard conversation — it's not really about the intern. Stop, notice, then choose. Inner awareness first, outward execution second — that pause is what makes the four moves below possible.

The four accountability moves

  • 1. State the reality — name the behaviour, the expectation, and the gap between them.
  • 2. Empathize AND expect — acknowledge their context AND restate the standard. Replace "but" with "and."
  • 3. Hand back responsibility — ask for their plan, not yours. Their words commit them in a way ours can't.
  • 4. Draw the boundary — be explicit about what you will and won't do, and why.

Judgment & Decision-Making Confidence The Escalation Readiness Test and the Decision Playbook

Session purpose: use structured reasoning before seeking help · distinguish discomfort from genuine risk · communicate decisions with precision and ownership · build confidence with ambiguity and incomplete data.

Good judgment in the PSA role is rarely about having perfect information — it's about acting responsibly under imperfect conditions. Hesitation usually comes from cognitive biases (risk aversion, seeking validation), emotional drivers (fear of being wrong, conflict avoidance), or system drivers (unclear SOPs, uneven expectations).

Key takeaway: good judgment = calibrated risk-taking + structured thinking + clear communication.

The Escalation Readiness Test

Before escalating, check all four:

  • Risk: is there reputational, legal, ethical, or program-threatening risk?
  • Action taken: have you taken two concrete, documented steps to resolve or de-escalate it yourself?
  • Evidence: can you present data, not assumptions — timestamps, quotes, deliverables, patterns?
  • Framing: is your escalation a recommendation with rationale, not a handoff or a plea for someone to "help me decide"?

Escalate when

There is legal, ethical, or reputational risk · two documented steps have been taken and the impact persists · the issue involves a policy conflict or systemic breakdown.

Do not escalate when

You're simply lacking confidence but have enough data · the issue is a performance matter within your authority · you're seeking validation, not guidance.

Decision Tree Exercise — "Escalate or Own?"

For a given scenario, build a decision tree considering: what signals actually matter, what data you're missing, what can be monitored instead of escalated, and what outcome you're optimizing for (intern growth, partner relationship, program integrity). Assign a confidence score (1–10) at each branch — this exposes your hidden uncertainty thresholds.

Decision Pathway Framework

  • 1. Define the core issue — what exactly is the problem?
  • 2. Gather key facts — what evidence do you have, what's missing?
  • 3. Identify 2–3 options
  • 4. Evaluate risks & impact — who's affected, how serious is it?

Reflection & growth commitments

Each PSA identifies: a decision type they'll now handle independently · their personal escalation rule · a plan for when an owned decision goes imperfectly (learning, not self-blame) · one bias to watch in themselves · what they need from the team to support better judgment.

Mediation & Conflict Resolution Why conflict happens, the mediation framework, written pushback

Session purpose: facilitate intern–HC conversations with confidence · apply a structured mediation flow · de-escalate conflict while maintaining trust and boundaries. This is not about being "nice" or "strict" — it's about being clear, structured, and fair.

Why conflict happens

Most conflicts fall into one or more categories: expectations gap, communication gap, information gap, skill–task misalignment, cultural differences, conflicting priorities, or capacity/workload constraints. Conflict is usually structural or developmental, not personal or malicious.

  • Structural — caused by the setup, not the person: unclear role/task scope, poor onboarding, timezone mismatch, conflicting incentives (academic vs. business deadlines).
  • Developmental — caused by learning gaps, not refusal or bad attitude: interns still learning professional norms (calendars, follow-ups, async work), supervisors not used to managing early-career talent, first-time remote work challenges.

The goal of mediation isn't to "keep everyone happy" — it's to surface facts, reduce misunderstanding, align expectations, and create a fair path forward. A successful outcome can include adjusted scope, clearer expectations, a monitored observation window, or — when needed — a respectful exit.

The mediation framework

  • 1. Gather information — never mediate on assumptions. Clarifying questions via email, 1:1 calls, 3-way calls when appropriate. Review intern side (CV, project plan, feedback tendencies) and HC side (hosting history, past feedback, communication norms).
  • 2. Identify the core issue — what's actually blocking progress? Is this skills, communication, expectations, or structure? Avoid treating symptoms instead of root causes — "the intern isn't performing well" might really mean tasks are too advanced, expectations were never clarified, or the intern doesn't know how to ask for help.
  • 3. Confirm key facts — meeting invites (timestamps), tasks assigned, response times, deliverables submitted, feedback already shared. This protects you when stories conflict, emotions escalate, or pushback happens later.
  • 4. Design middle-ground solutions — identify the specific gap, propose an option that balances both sides, prepare multiple alternatives rather than just one. Choice increases buy-in and reduces defensiveness.
  • 5. Communicate resolution clearly — set a 1–2 week observation window. Define what improvement looks like, what actions are expected, when follow-up will occur. Monitor patterns, not single incidents.
  • 6. Follow up and monitor — not optional. Check for patterns, document outcomes, step in quickly if issues repeat.

Mediation calls — live application

Before: review facts and context, prepare potential solutions, define your boundaries. During: lead the conversation calmly, give space for both sides to speak, ask clarifying questions, summarize understanding out loud, check alignment explicitly. After: confirm agreements/next steps in writing, set the next check-in, document clearly.

In practice, mediation is affected by culture, bias, emotional states, personal communication styles, and language/connectivity barriers. It's okay to deviate slightly from the framework or adjust pacing — the framework is a base, not a constraint. Losing clarity is not okay.

Written mediation — email pushback & boundary setting

Typical flow: acknowledge the concern and show understanding → express curiosity, invite clarification if needed → offer support with realistic solutions → set boundaries clearly, referencing policy or process → strengthen your stance if initial pushback isn't accepted.

You may need to shift tone mid-exchange: start warm → get firmer, or start firm → soften slightly, explanatory → directive, or switch from email to a call to reduce friction. Keep mediation timelines tight — don't let it drag beyond 1–2 weeks, and reassess/follow up within 24–48 hours.

Dos and don'ts

Do: stay neutral and fact-based, validate emotions without assigning blame, set clear boundaries aligned with policy, document outcomes.
Don't: escalate to avoid discomfort, delay decisions to avoid risk, over-promise outcomes, skip observation windows.

When mediation isn't working

Mediation may fail when one side disengages completely, safety or compliance is at risk, commitments are repeatedly broken, or there's no willingness to adjust. At that point: document clearly, align internally, communicate closure respectfully.

Key takeaways

Most conflicts stem from gaps, not bad intent. Facts reduce emotion. Conflicts can be resolved with structure and clarity. Documentation protects all parties. Tone and structure matter as much as content. Strong mediation builds trust even when outcomes are imperfect.

The mindset the five rules run on: agency

Task Owner (low agency): asked what to do at every step, brings questions instead of options, waits to be unblocked, escalates uncertainty.
Trusted Operator (high agency): creates direction when it's missing, solves problems before escalating, uses judgment when the SOP runs out, focuses on outcomes.

The five rules

  • 1. No-Orphan Rule — every outcome has an owner, problems don't float. In practice: every case in the Queue has a named next action and a date, not "monitor." Every HubSpot log names who owns the next move. Every hand-off includes what you tried and what you recommend.
  • 2. Thinking Tax Rule — before you ask for help, try first. Come with options, not just a question. When escalating: what did you observe, what did you try, what do you recommend? The number alone lies — a WFL of 7 can sit on top of weeks of chaos. You are the programme's primary truth-finding mechanism, not the dashboard.
  • 3. No-Vibe Rule — before you start, know what excellent looks like. Not what task you're completing, but what outcome you're trying to create. Being busy and being effective aren't the same thing.
  • 4. Green Light Rule — not every decision needs escalation; when you see something broken, fix it. Before acting, ask: is this mine to resolve, or am I moving it because it's uncomfortable? Does it affect someone else's work or a relationship I don't own — if so, flag as information, not for permission. Can this be undone if I'm wrong — if not, escalate first.
  • 5. Last Mile Rule — sent is not done. Done means the outcome was reached. A WFL follow-up isn't complete because a reminder was sent — it's complete when feedback comes back and something meaningful is done with it. A supervisor outreach isn't done because an email went out — it's done when the situation is understood, a response received, a next step named.

What does a PSA do?

PSAs manage the during-internship experience: running structured check-in calls, monitoring weekly feedback scores, identifying risks, ensuring smooth communication, and building trusted relationships with both interns and host companies.

How the team fits together: Pre-Internship (PMs/PRS) match interns with host companies and prepare interns for placement — comparable to Talent Acquisition. During Internship (PSAs) support interns and HCs throughout the internship itself — comparable to HR/People Operations.

PSAs sit in the messy middle — constantly balancing three simultaneous pulls, rarely with perfect clarity handed to them. Navigation, trade-offs, and relationship management under pressure are core to the role.

Vision, mission & values

Vision: We empower interns and host companies to succeed by turning every challenge into an opportunity for growth, connection, and measurable impact.

Mission: To protect and improve the remote internship experience by ensuring every intern and host company has the clarity, support, and consistency needed for success.

  • Resourcefulness: We navigate incomplete info and messy situations with persistence and creativity.
  • Candor with care: We speak openly and honestly, balancing clarity with respect.
  • Progress over perfection: Small wins compound into lasting impact, even when conditions aren't perfect.
  • Teamwork across time zones: Our strength is in supporting each other, always.
